Modern Greek vs Ancient Greek: Which Should You Learn?

Getting startedPublished 14 July 20262 min read

Both are “Greek”, but they are used for different goals. Modern Greek is a living language you can speak today. Ancient Greek is a written language for reading classical, philosophical and biblical texts.

The short answer

  • Choose Modern Greek if you want to travel, talk with Greeks, watch films, read the news, or connect with family.
  • Choose Ancient Greek if you want to read Plato, Homer, Sophocles or the New Testament in the original.

How the language changed

Greek has one of the longest recorded histories of any European language, spanning over 3,000 years. It is usually divided into stages:

StageApproximate periodKnown for
Ancient Greek (Classical)5th to 4th century BCHomer, Plato, Aristotle, the Athenian playwrights
Koine (common) Greek4th century BC to 4th century ADThe New Testament, a shared language across the Mediterranean
Medieval (Byzantine) Greek4th to 15th centuryThe Byzantine Empire
Modern GreekFrom the 15th century onwardsToday's spoken and written Greek

Main differences

  • Grammar: Ancient Greek has five cases (including the dative), a dual number and more complex verb forms. Modern Greek has four cases, no dative, and simpler verbs.
  • Pronunciation: Many sounds merged over time. Modern Greek pronounces η, ι, υ, ει, οι all as “ee”. Ancient pronunciation differed, and scholars debate the details.
  • Accents: Ancient texts use several accents and breathing marks (the polytonic system). Modern Greek officially adopted the simple one-accent system (monotonic) in 1982.
  • Vocabulary: Many words survived, some changed meaning, and Modern Greek has also borrowed from Italian, Turkish, French and English.

How much can a Modern Greek speaker understand?

A Modern Greek speaker can often recognise many words in ancient texts, and Greeks study Ancient Greek at school. But reading Plato still takes real study, a bit like an English speaker reading Old English. Going the other way, ancient-language learners who then go to Greece often struggle to speak because pronunciation and vocabulary differ.

Katharevousa and Demotic

For much of the 19th and 20th centuries Greece had two forms: the formal, archaising Καθαρεύουσα (Katharevousa) and the everyday Δημοτική (Demotic). Demotic became the official language of the state and education in 1976. The Greek you hear today and learn from modern courses is Standard Modern Greek, based on Demotic.

Which first? Many students of Ancient Greek benefit from learning Modern Greek basics first, because the alphabet and many roots carry over. Learning the alphabet is the shared first step. See our weekend alphabet plan.
